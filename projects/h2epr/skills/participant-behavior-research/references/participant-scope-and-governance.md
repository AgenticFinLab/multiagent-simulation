# Participant scope and institutional governance

The first modeling decision is what the Agent represents. Historical names do
not by themselves define a decision-making unit.

## Representation statement

Write a representation statement covering:

| Question | Required explanation |
|---|---|
| Historical entity | The person, office, firm, association, committee, or public body present in the event. |
| Modeled decision interface | The unit whose observations, state, alternatives, and intents the Agent represents. |
| Authorized voice | Who or what process can speak, decide, request, approve, reject, or commit on behalf of the entity. |
| Included actors | Individuals, offices, committees, members, or routines aggregated into the Agent. |
| Excluded actors | Parties represented elsewhere, treated as scenario processes, or outside the model scope. |
| Aggregation rationale | Why the included actors can be combined for the focal research question. |
| Suppressed heterogeneity | Conflicts, factions, delays, or information differences lost through aggregation. |
| Split trigger | Evidence or process patterns that would require a finer actor model. |

Do not use the institution's legal name as evidence that it had a single
preference, belief, memory, or action channel.

## Candidate representation levels

### Individual participant

Use when the research question concerns a named person's authority, knowledge,
judgment, negotiation, or action and sources can distinguish that person from
the institution.

Do not infer private psychology from an institutional outcome. A biography,
public reputation, or later recollection may inform hypotheses but does not
automatically establish decision-time belief.

### Office or authorized decision role

Use when an office carries stable authority and information that matter more
than the office holder's personal traits. Record appointment, delegation,
reporting, term, and procedural constraints where material.

### Aggregate institutional Agent

Use when the focal decisions can be represented through an institutional
interface with explicit governance and the internal differences do not change
the process patterns under study.

An aggregate institutional Agent should expose:

- mandate and institutional obligations;
- eligibility and membership rules;
- decision and authorization procedures;
- information supplied to the authorized interface;
- resources it owns, controls, coordinates, or can only request;
- persistent case, review, authorization, or commitment state;
- permissible communications and action intents;
- known aggregation limits.

### Institution plus internal actors

Use only when internal disagreement, delegation, committee sequence, or
asymmetric information generates distinguishable predictions that an aggregate
interface cannot express. More Agents are justified by explanatory need, not
by a general belief that greater granularity is more realistic.

### Scenario-owned institutional process

Some procedures may be deterministic or exogenous within the current research
scope. Treat them as scenario-owned when there is no independently modeled
choice, memory, or behavior to explain. State which causal questions this
choice leaves unanswered.

## Governance analysis

Distinguish:

1. **Formal mandate** — chartered or legally stated purpose and duties.
2. **Formal authority** — actions the entity or body could authorize.
3. **Eligibility and jurisdiction** — persons or institutions to which that
   authority applied.
4. **Decision procedure** — notice, quorum, examination, consultation,
   voting, delegation, approval, or review requirements.
5. **Resource control** — ownership, custody, access, coordination, or the
   power only to request another party's resource.
6. **Observed practice** — what the institution actually did in comparable or
   focal situations.
7. **Informal discretion** — practices, exceptions, influence, negotiation,
   or norms not reducible to formal text.
8. **Modeled simplification** — what the Agent compresses for the current
   question.

A formal rule and observed practice may conflict. Preserve the conflict and
model its consequences; do not silently choose whichever explains the known
outcome.

## Goals, duties, and preferences

Institutional behavior is often constrained before it is optimized. Analyze in
this order:

1. prohibitions and jurisdiction;
2. mandatory duties and procedural preconditions;
3. existing commitments and resource constraints;
4. role-specific objectives and priorities;
5. discretion among the remaining legal alternatives;
6. fallback, escalation, delay, and abstention.

Generic `risk tolerance`, `confidence`, or `willingness` is insufficient when
membership, authorization, review, or resource control explains the choice.
Such constructs are acceptable only when defined, sourced, behaviorally
necessary, and distinguishable from the institutional process.

## Relationship boundary

For each material relationship, record:

- counterpart and institutional basis;
- direction and type of authority, obligation, service, dependence, or
  communication;
- information conveyed and its delay;
- resource or action enabled;
- event-time validity;
- whether the relationship is participant knowledge or only world state;
- source and uncertainty.

The Agent interprets a relationship and may act through it. The scenario owns
the actual network, delivery, counterpart state, and resulting effect.

## Representation sufficiency review

Ask:

- Can the Agent's authority be explained without personifying the institution?
- Can a reviewer identify who is allowed to issue each intent?
- Are internal states organizationally meaningful rather than generic emotions?
- Does aggregation preserve the sequences and conflicts relevant to the focal
  decisions?
- Is every important omitted actor or process named?
- Would splitting the Agent produce a different, testable process prediction?
- Would collapsing it further lose a necessary decision or information
  boundary?

Choose the least complex representation that preserves the distinctions needed
to answer the research question.
