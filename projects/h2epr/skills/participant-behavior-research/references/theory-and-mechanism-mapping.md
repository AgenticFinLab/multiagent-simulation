# Theory and mechanism mapping

Theory gives a vocabulary for candidate mechanisms. Historical and empirical
evidence determines whether that vocabulary is appropriate for the participant
and event.

## Evidence roles

Keep these roles distinct:

| Evidence role | Contribution | Does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| General theory | A causal or decision mechanism and its conditions. | That the named participant used it. |
| Empirical study | A measured pattern or relation in a defined population and period. | Direct transfer to another institution or event. |
| Event-specific evidence | Identity, information, authority, practice, state, action, or relation in the focal event. | A universal behavioral rule. |
| Parameter reconstruction | A bounded quantity, ordering, or state estimate. | Mechanism validity by itself. |
| Historical interpretation | A scholarly synthesis of causes or institutional meaning. | Uncontested fact or decision-time knowledge. |
| Analogy | A candidate explanation or contrast. | Historical equivalence. |
| Modeling assumption | A transparent completion needed to study the question. | Evidence support. |

Use original theoretical and empirical sources for claims that enter the
publication-facing dossier. Reviews and advisory reports may organize the
landscape and identify citations, but should not become the only support for a
mechanism.

## Mechanism record

For each candidate mechanism, record:

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Mechanism name | A descriptive label, not a code identifier. |
| Explanatory target | Decision or process pattern the mechanism addresses. |
| General basis | Original theory or empirical sources and the scope they support. |
| Event applicability | Claims showing why the mechanism may operate for this participant, institution, and interval. |
| Decision carrier | Person, office, committee, institutional process, or aggregate interface through which it operates. |
| Legal inputs | Observations and prior state on which it may depend. |
| Causal process | How those inputs and constraints alter alternatives, precedence, or intent. |
| Boundary conditions | Situations in which the mechanism should not apply. |
| Competing mechanisms | Alternatives that could explain the same behavior or trace. |
| Parameters | Quantities or qualitative distinctions required and their evidence status. |
| Observable implications | Process patterns, choices, delays, messages, or state changes expected if the mechanism matters. |
| Falsifier | Evidence or perturbation that would reject or narrow it. |
| Unresolved elements | Unknown institutional, informational, or quantitative features. |

## Translation discipline

Make the evidence-to-model step visible:

```text
source-backed claim
    -> historical or institutional interpretation
    -> candidate behavioral mechanism
    -> decision-situation consequence
    -> observable prediction or falsifier
```

Each arrow is an argument. A citation at the first step does not validate all
later steps.

For example, evidence that an institution served members and restricted a
particular facility to members may constrain its available support routes. It
does not by itself establish a universal preference to reject all nonmembers,
the absence of informal discretion, or a precise probability of support.

## Competing mechanisms

Preserve alternatives when they imply different structures. Common examples
include:

- formal ineligibility versus discretionary member priority;
- resource insufficiency versus unwillingness to commit resources;
- missing information versus adverse assessment;
- procedural delay versus strategic delay;
- individual discretion versus committee or member coordination;
- public signaling versus private operational action;
- bounded attention versus intentionally selective response.

Do not collapse these into a weighted average unless the research question,
evidence, and model can interpret such weights. Prefer parallel mechanism
statements, explicit unknowns, or sensitivity variants.

## Parameter analysis

For each parameter or qualitative threshold, state:

- behavioral meaning;
- unit, ordering, category, or range;
- whether it represents the world, the participant's observation, private
  state, or a behavioral response;
- source and reconstruction method;
- event and participant-time relevance;
- identified, bounded, assumed, sensitivity-only, disputed, or unknown status;
- mechanism and decisions affected;
- expected direction of change;
- observational or structural alternatives that cannot be represented by
  changing the number.

Use exact values only when their precision is meaningful. An ordinal condition
such as insufficient, uncertain, under review, or not authorized may be more
faithful and more testable than an invented continuous score.

## Causal sufficiency and restraint

Ask whether the mechanism adds explanatory content beyond the known outcome.
A useful mechanism should help predict at least one of:

- what information the participant seeks or ignores;
- when it delays, abstains, escalates, requests, authorizes, refuses, or
  communicates;
- how authority or membership changes its available actions;
- how pending commitments affect later decisions;
- how behavior changes under a role, information, resource, or procedure
  perturbation;
- what trace pattern should not occur.

A mechanism that only restates “the participant did what history records” is
not a behavioral explanation.

## Review questions

- Does the original source support the mechanism attributed to it?
- Are population, period, institution, and decision conditions transferable?
- Is event applicability supported, assumed, or disputed?
- Could a simpler institutional or informational explanation account for the
  same behavior?
- Do competing mechanisms make distinguishable predictions?
- Are precise values justified, or do they hide structural uncertainty?
- Can the mechanism fail?
- Can a reader see what would change if a supporting claim were withdrawn?
